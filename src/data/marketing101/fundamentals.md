# MARKETING 101 — FUNDAMENTALS

**What this document is:** the permanent layer. Principles and decision rules that were true in 1960 and will be true in 2040. Channels change; none of this does.

**How to use it:** this is not reference material to be summarized. It is a decision procedure. Given a business, work top to bottom: diagnose, then prescribe. Never prescribe before diagnosing.

**Scope:** these rules apply identically to a $600B retailer and a taco truck. The diagnosis is the same. Only the budget and the channel changes.

---

## PART 1 — THE DIAGNOSIS

Never recommend a tactic before you know where the business is leaking. Most marketing fails because someone bought traffic for a business with a conversion problem, or ran a promotion for a business with a retention problem.

### The five leaks

Every business that isn't growing is leaking in one of five places. Find the leak first.

**1. Awareness leak** — Not enough people know they exist.
Signal: low traffic, low search volume for their brand name, few reviews, owner says "people don't know we're here."
Test: if you sent 100 ideal customers to their door tomorrow, would they convert? If yes, it's awareness.

**2. Conversion leak** — People find them and don't buy.
Signal: decent traffic, weak sales. High foot traffic, low ticket. Website visits with no calls. Owner says "we get lookers, not buyers."
Test: what percentage of people who make contact become customers? Below industry norm means conversion.

**3. Retention leak** — People buy once and never return.
Signal: high customer acquisition cost with flat revenue. Owner can't name a regular. No email or SMS list.
Test: what percentage of this month's revenue came from someone who bought before? Under 30% for most local businesses is a retention leak.

**4. Pricing leak** — They sell plenty and make nothing.
Signal: busy but broke. This is the most common leak in local business and the one nobody diagnoses.
Test: gross margin per transaction. If a 10% price increase would lose fewer than 10% of customers, they are underpriced. It almost always would.

**5. Positioning leak** — They're competing on the wrong axis.
Signal: constant price pressure, competing with everyone, no reason to choose them.
Test: can the owner say in one sentence why someone should pick them over the place down the street, without saying "quality" or "service"? If no, positioning.

### The diagnostic order

Run them in this order, because fixing a later leak while an earlier one is open wastes money — but fixing an earlier one while a later one is open wastes *more*.

1. **Positioning** — if they can't say why they're the choice, no amount of traffic helps.
2. **Pricing** — if the unit economics don't work, growth accelerates the loss.
3. **Conversion** — if they don't convert, traffic is a tax.
4. **Retention** — if they don't retain, acquisition is a treadmill.
5. **Awareness** — only now is it safe to pour traffic in.

**The rule that follows from this:** most people hire a marketer for awareness. Most businesses need 1 through 4 first. Say so. Fixing conversion is faster, cheaper, and more profitable than buying traffic, and it makes the traffic worth buying later.

### The diagnostic questions

Ask these of any business, any size:

- What's your average transaction value?
- What does a customer spend with you over a year?
- Where does your business come from today, ranked?
- What percentage of customers come back?
- What do you charge, and when did you last raise it?
- Who's your closest competitor, and why do people pick you over them?
- If I doubled your customers tomorrow, could you serve them?
- What's your gross margin?
- How many people who contact you become customers?
- What's the single biggest constraint on growth right now?

That last question is the one that reveals whether marketing is even the answer. Sometimes it's capacity. Sometimes it's the owner. Marketing a business that can't fulfill is malpractice.

## PART 2 — THE OFFER

The offer is the highest-leverage object in marketing. A great offer with mediocre copy on a mediocre channel beats a mediocre offer with great copy everywhere. Most marketers skip straight to channels because channels are visible and offers are hard.

### What an offer actually is

Not the product. The offer is the complete proposition: what they get, what they pay, what they risk, and why now.

### The four components

**1. The dream outcome.** What they actually want. Not the product — the result. Nobody wants a website; they want customers who find them. Nobody wants tacos; they want twenty minutes of pleasure and a full stomach for eight dollars.

**2. Perceived likelihood of achievement.** Do they believe it'll work for them specifically? This is what proof, guarantees, and specificity address.

**3. Time delay.** How long until they get it? Shorter is always better. If it's inherently long, manufacture early wins.

**4. Effort and sacrifice.** What do they have to do? Every step you remove increases conversion. Done-for-you beats done-with-you beats do-it-yourself, and prices accordingly.

Increase 1 and 2. Decrease 3 and 4. That's offer construction, entirely.

### Offer strengthening moves, in order of power

- **Risk reversal.** Guarantee. The stronger and more specific, the better. "Money back" is weak. "If you don't book 10 extra jobs in 60 days, you don't pay, and you keep the website" is strong. Guarantees increase conversion more than they increase refunds — this is consistently true and consistently unbelieved by business owners.
- **Specificity.** "More customers" is nothing. "12 to 18 more catering bookings a month" is an offer. Specific numbers outperform vague benefits every single time.
- **Bonuses.** Stack value against the price. Bonuses should solve the *next* problem the customer will have after buying.
- **Scarcity and urgency.** Real only. Fake urgency works once and costs you the relationship. Real scarcity: limited capacity, seasonal window, price increase on a date.
- **Naming.** A named offer outperforms an unnamed one. "The Catering Engine" beats "our marketing package."
- **Payment structure.** Splitting a price into terms, or moving to a deposit, changes conversion more than lowering the price does.

### Pricing rules

- Price is a positioning signal before it's a number. Cheap says "commodity."
- Almost every local business is underpriced. Test a raise before you spend a dollar on ads.
- Never compete on price unless you have a structural cost advantage. You don't.
- Anchor high. The first number seen sets the frame for every number after.
- Offer three tiers. Most buyers take the middle. The top tier's job is often to make the middle look reasonable, and occasionally someone buys it, which is free money.
- Charge for outcomes, not hours, wherever you can.
- Raising price on the same volume is the fastest profit lever that exists. It requires no marketing spend at all.

## PART 3 — POSITIONING

### The one-sentence test

The business should be able to complete: *"We're the [category] for [specific customer] who want [specific outcome], unlike [alternative] which [limitation]."*

If they can't, everything downstream is mush.

### Positioning rules

- **Specific beats broad, always.** The narrower the position, the easier every subsequent marketing decision becomes. "Marketing agency" competes with 50,000 firms. "Marketing for local food trucks" competes with almost nobody, and every food truck owner instantly self-identifies.
- **Position against an alternative, not in a vacuum.** People understand you by contrast.
- **Own one attribute.** Fastest, cheapest, most specialized, most convenient, most premium. One. Trying to own several means owning none.
- **Do not claim quality or service.** Every business claims those. They communicate nothing.
- **The best positioning is often a limitation.** "We only do X." Constraints signal expertise.
- **Positioning is a business decision disguised as a marketing decision.** It determines who you turn away. If nobody is turned away, you have no position.

### Category design

The strongest position is being the only one in your category. If you can't win the category, subdivide it until you can. "Best mechanic in town" is a fight. "The only mobile mechanic who comes to office parking lots during the workday" is a monopoly.

## PART 4 — THE CUSTOMER

### Rules

- **Talk to them.** Ten conversations with real customers beat any amount of demographic data. The exact words they use become your copy.
- **Buy the emotion, justify with logic.** People decide emotionally and rationalize afterward. Lead with the feeling, supply the logic for the rationalization.
- **Find the trigger event.** Almost nobody wakes up wanting to buy. Something happened. The pipe burst, the lease came up, the photo looked bad, the competitor opened. Marketing that intercepts the trigger event converts at multiples of marketing that doesn't.
- **Know what they were doing five minutes before.** This determines the channel and the hook more than demographics do.
- **Segment by behavior, not demographics.** "People who have bought in the last 90 days" is a more useful segment than "women 35–44."

### The awareness ladder

Every prospect sits on one of five rungs. The message must match the rung. Mismatching is the single most common copy failure.

1. **Unaware** — doesn't know they have a problem. Needs education and story.
2. **Problem aware** — knows the problem, not the solution. Needs the problem named vividly, then a solution category introduced.
3. **Solution aware** — knows solutions exist, doesn't know you. Needs differentiation.
4. **Product aware** — knows you, hasn't bought. Needs proof and risk reversal.
5. **Most aware** — ready. Needs the offer and a deadline.

Cold traffic is mostly rungs 1–3. Retargeting is 3–4. Your email list is 4–5. Running rung-5 copy at rung-1 traffic is why most ads fail.

## PART 5 — MESSAGE AND COPY

### The hook

The first three seconds, the subject line, the headline, the first frame. It has one job: earn the next line. Nothing else.

Hook rules:
- Specificity beats cleverness. Clever is a trap; it feels like good work and doesn't sell.
- Lead with the customer's problem or desired outcome, never with your company name.
- Numbers, timeframes, and named places outperform adjectives.
- Curiosity works but must be paid off immediately or you train people to ignore you.
- The best hook is often a plain sentence that names the exact situation the reader is in.

### Copy rules

- **One idea per piece.** Multiple messages equal no message.
- **Write to one person.** "You," singular.
- **Features tell, benefits sell, but proof closes.** Every claim needs evidence next to it.
- **Cut every sentence that isn't doing work.** First drafts are 40% too long.
- **Read it aloud.** If you stumble, rewrite it.
- **Use their words, not industry words.** Mine reviews, call transcripts, and comment sections for exact phrasing.
- **Concrete over abstract.** "Fixed in two hours" not "fast, reliable service."
- **The call to action must be one specific action.** Multiple CTAs halve response.

### Proof, ranked by power

1. Results with numbers, attached to a named person
2. Video testimonial
3. Before and after
4. Written testimonial with full name and photo
5. Volume of reviews and star rating
6. Case study
7. Client logos
8. Credentials and years in business
9. Awards

Most businesses have proof and never collect it. Building a proof collection habit is worth more than most campaigns.

## PART 6 — CHANNEL ECONOMICS

### The only equation that matters

**LTV > CAC**, with enough margin and fast enough payback that the business doesn't die of cash flow while waiting.

- **LTV** — what a customer is worth over their lifetime, in gross profit, not revenue.
- **CAC** — everything spent to get one customer, divided by customers acquired. Including your time.
- **Payback period** — how long until CAC is recovered. For a small business with no capital, this matters more than the ratio. A 5:1 LTV:CAC that takes 14 months to pay back will kill a taco truck.

Rules:
- 3:1 LTV:CAC is healthy for most businesses. Below 2:1, something is broken.
- If payback is under 30 days, spend everything you can.
- Know these numbers before recommending any paid channel. If the client doesn't know them, that's the first deliverable.

### Channel selection principles

- **Go where they already are.** Not where you're comfortable. Not where it's trendy.
- **Match intent to channel.** Search captures existing demand. Social creates it. Emergency services live on search. Discretionary purchases live on social and word of mouth.
- **Local beats broad for local businesses.** A 3-mile radius with 500 impressions beats a national campaign with 50,000.
- **Owned beats rented.** Email lists, SMS lists, and customer databases are assets. Social followers are rented from a platform that can change the rules tomorrow. Every campaign should be converting rented attention into owned attention.
- **One channel, done properly, beats five done badly.** Especially under $2,000/month. Concentration wins at low budgets.
- **Organic and paid compound each other.** Paid finds what works, organic scales it cheaply, organic proof makes paid convert.
- **Test small, then concentrate.** Small tests across a few channels, then move everything to the winner. Do not maintain a losing channel for the sake of presence.

### Budget allocation heuristics

- Under $500/month: one channel, plus free fundamentals (Google Business Profile, reviews, referrals). Paid ads at this level rarely clear the noise floor.
- $500–2,000/month: one paid channel plus one organic, plus owned-list building.
- $2,000–10,000/month: two to three channels, with retargeting always as one of them.
- Above $10,000: portfolio approach, formal testing budget of 10–20%.

### The free plays that outperform paid spend

For local businesses, these consistently beat early paid spend and are systematically overlooked:

- **Google Business Profile, fully completed and actively posted to.** For most local businesses this is the single highest-ROI marketing asset in existence and it costs nothing. Most businesses have it half-filled or unclaimed.
- **A review generation system.** Asking every customer, at the right moment, with a direct link. Going from 12 reviews to 90 changes a business's economics more than any ad campaign.
- **Reactivating past customers.** The cheapest sale is to someone who already bought. Most local businesses have never once contacted a past customer.
- **A referral ask.** Structured, specific, incentivized. Not "tell your friends."
- **Cross-promotion with a complementary local business.** Zero cost, shared audience, immediate.
- **Answering the phone.** A meaningful share of local businesses lose more revenue to missed calls than to weak marketing. Audit this before spending anything.

## PART 7 — CONVERSION

Traffic without conversion is a tax. Fix conversion before buying traffic.

### The conversion checklist for any local business

- Does the phone get answered, every time, during stated hours?
- Is there a way to book or order without talking to a human?
- Does the website load in under 3 seconds on a phone?
- Is the phone number tappable and visible without scrolling?
- Are hours accurate everywhere they appear?
- Is there proof above the fold?
- Is there one clear next action per page?
- Is the offer stated in the first screen?
- Are there photos of the actual thing, not stock?
- How long between a lead coming in and someone responding?

That last one is enormous and almost universally ignored. Response speed to inbound leads is one of the strongest predictors of close rate in existence. Minutes matter; hours are fatal.

### Friction removal

Every step between wanting and buying loses people. Count the steps. Remove half. Then remove half again.

## PART 8 — RETENTION AND THE COMPOUND

- Acquiring a new customer costs several times more than retaining one. Universally true, universally ignored.
- A small increase in retention produces a disproportionate increase in profit, because retained customers cost nothing to reacquire and buy more over time.
- **Build the list from day one.** Email and SMS. Every transaction should have a capture point.
- **Frequency beats reach for existing customers.** Show up consistently to people who already know you.
- **The follow-up sequence is where most revenue is left.** Most businesses contact a lead once. Most sales happen after multiple touches.
- **A reactivation campaign to past customers is the fastest revenue in marketing.** It should be the first campaign you run for almost any established business, before any acquisition spend.

## PART 9 — MEASUREMENT

### The rules

- **Measure what leads to money.** Impressions and followers are not money. Leads, bookings, and revenue are.
- **Attribution is always imperfect.** Accept it. Use directional data and holdout tests rather than chasing perfect attribution.
- **Ask every customer how they heard about you, and record it.** Crude, unglamorous, and more useful than most analytics.
- **Establish the baseline before you start.** Without a before, there is no after.
- **Give a channel enough time and volume to be judged.** Killing a channel after 4 days and $60 tells you nothing.
- **One variable at a time**, or you learn nothing from the result.

### The metrics that matter, by leak

- Awareness: reach, brand search volume, new customer count
- Conversion: contact-to-customer rate, page conversion rate, response time
- Retention: repeat purchase rate, revenue from returning customers
- Pricing: gross margin, average transaction value
- Positioning: win rate against named competitors, price resistance frequency

## PART 10 — SCALE AND SIZE

The principles are identical across business size. What changes:

**Small business (taco truck, local trades, salon)**
- Payback period dominates. Cash flow is the constraint.
- Free and owned channels first. Paid only after the free plays are exhausted.
- The owner is the brand. Use them.
- Local density beats reach.
- One channel done well.

**Mid-market**
- Systems and repeatability matter more than individual campaigns.
- Now worth building a testing budget and a real measurement stack.
- Brand starts to compound; it's no longer all direct response.

**Enterprise (Walmart-scale)**
- Brand and distribution dominate over campaign cleverness.
- Small percentage improvements on huge volume are worth more than any single creative idea.
- Long time horizons are affordable, so brand investment pays.
- The constraint is usually organizational, not creative.

**The one thing that scales down but not up:** speed and personality. A taco truck can post a video from the line and get customers that afternoon. Walmart cannot. Use that advantage relentlessly for small clients — it's the only structural edge they have.

**The one thing that scales up but not down:** patience. Big companies can wait 18 months. Never build a plan for a small business that requires waiting.

## PART 11 — THINGS MOST MARKETERS GET WRONG

Stated plainly, because these are where the money is:

- Selling awareness to a business with a conversion problem.
- Recommending the newest channel instead of the fitting one.
- Ignoring price as a lever because it isn't "marketing."
- Building a beautiful website for a business that doesn't answer its phone.
- Never asking for reviews.
- Never contacting past customers.
- Confusing activity with progress. Posting daily is not a strategy.
- Chasing followers instead of building an owned list.
- Optimizing the ad when the offer is the problem.
- Testing five things at once and learning nothing.
- Quitting a working channel out of boredom.
- Believing what the client says about their customers instead of asking the customers.
- Not knowing the client's margin before spending their money.
- Treating a slow lead response as an operations problem rather than the marketing problem it is.

## PART 12 — THE DECISION PROCEDURE

Given any business, in order:

1. Get the numbers: transaction value, margin, LTV, current sources of business, capacity.
2. Diagnose the leak. Positioning, pricing, conversion, retention, awareness — in that order.
3. Fix the earliest open leak first. Say so out loud even if they asked for something else.
4. Construct or strengthen the offer before touching any channel.
5. Confirm the message matches the awareness rung of the traffic you're about to buy.
6. Exhaust the free plays: Google Business Profile, reviews, reactivation, referrals, cross-promotion.
7. Fix conversion and response time.
8. Only now select a channel — where their customers already are, matched to intent, concentrated not spread.
9. Set the baseline, launch small, one variable, enough time.
10. Convert rented attention into owned attention on every single campaign.
11. Measure to revenue. Kill or scale on data, not feeling.
12. Reinvest into what worked. Concentration compounds; diversification at small scale dilutes.

If you can only do one thing for a business, it is almost never an ad. It is usually the offer, the price, the Google Business Profile, or the phone.
